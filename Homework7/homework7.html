2. Theoretical Concepts & Statistical Interpretation

Mathematical Definition of Independence

In probability theory and statistics, two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are statistically independent if knowing the value of one provides no information about the distribution of the other. Formally, their joint probability equals the product of their marginal probabilities for all pairs $(x_i, y_j)$:
$$P(X = x_i \cap Y = y_j) = P(X = x_i) \cdot P(Y = y_j)$$

Equivalently, in terms of conditional probability, the distribution of $Y$ given $X = x_i$ is identical to the unconditional marginal distribution of $Y$:
$$P(Y = y_j \mid X = x_i) = \frac{P(X = x_i \cap Y = y_j)}{P(X = x_i)} = P(Y = y_j)$$

Expected Frequencies & Chi-Square Statistic ($\chi^2$)

In an empirical contingency table of size $N$, the expected frequency $E_{ij}$ under the null hypothesis of independence ($H_0$) is computed from the row marginal sum $R_i$ and column marginal sum $C_j$:
$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{N}$$

The Pearson Chi-Square statistic ($\chi^2$) measures the overall discrepancy between observed frequencies ($O_{ij}$) and expected frequencies ($E_{ij}$):
$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$ with degrees of freedom $df = (r - 1)(c - 1)$. A small $p$-value ($p < 0.05$) leads us to reject $H_0$, indicating statistically significant dependence between $X$ and $Y$.

3. Discussion: Statistical Association vs. Causality

When the Chi-square test indicates statistical dependence, it confirms that $X$ and $Y$ are associated (they co-vary in a non-random way). However, statistical dependence alone does not prove a cause-and-effect relationship:

  • Confounding Variables (Lurking Factors): A third unobserved variable $Z$ may simultaneously influence both $X$ and $Y$, creating a spurious correlation between them without any direct causal link.
  • Directionality Ambiguity: Contingency tables and $\chi^2$ tests are symmetric—swapping $X$ and $Y$ yields the exact same $\chi^2$ value. Statistical association cannot tell whether $X$ causes $Y$ or $Y$ causes $X$.
  • Establishing Causality: Moving from association to causality requires controlled randomized experiments, temporal precedence (cause precedes effect), or formal causal inference frameworks (such as do-calculus and Structural Causal Models) to isolate interventions from observational correlations.
