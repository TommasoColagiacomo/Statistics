Homework Exploration: Riemann vs. Lebesgue Integration & Computation of the Standard Normal CDF

Homework 1: Riemann vs. Lebesgue Integration

This module evaluates definite integrals numerically using two distinct mathematical formulations:

  • Riemann Integration: Partitions the domain $[a, b]$ into $N$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/N$ and sums rectangle areas using midpoint evaluation: $\sum f(x_i^*) \Delta x$.
  • Lebesgue Integration: Partitions the range $[y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$ into $M$ horizontal slices of height $\Delta y$, measures the measure $\mu(E_j)$ of preimages $E_j = \{x \in [a,b] : y_j \le f(x) < y_{j+1}\}$, and computes $\sum y_j^* \cdot \mu(E_j)$.

Theoretical Comparison & Findings

  • Continuous & Piecewise Continuous Functions (Ex. 1–4, 6, 7): Both Riemann and Lebesgue numerical approximations converge smoothly to the exact theoretical values as partition size increases. Riemann approaches grid alignment horizontally along the domain axis, whereas Lebesgue sums vertical slices of preimages.
  • Unbounded Singular Integrals (Ex. 5: $1/\sqrt{x}$): The function is improper in the Riemann sense because $f(x) \to \infty$ at $x \to 0$. Lebesgue numerical integration handles singular range partitioning natively by capping at $y_{\max}$ and integrating bounded horizontal slices, demonstrating greater stability.
  • Pathological Functions (Ex. 8 & 9):
    • Dirichlet Function (Ex. 8): $f(x) = 1$ if $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, $0$ if $x \notin \mathbb{Q}$. On computers, floating-point numbers are rational, so a naive evaluation yields $1$, giving a Riemann sum of $1.0$. Mathematically, rationals have Lebesgue measure $\mu(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]) = 0$, so the true Lebesgue integral is $0$.
    • Thomae's Function (Ex. 9): Continuous at every irrational point and discontinuous at every rational point. Its Lebesgue integral is identically $0$ because non-zero values occur on a set of measure zero.

Homework 2: Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution $Z \sim N(0, 1)$ is defined as: $$\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \right]$$

Mathematical Foundation (Liouville's Theorem) & Error Function

By Liouville's Theorem on Integration in Finite Terms (Rosenlicht, 1972), the integrand $e^{-t^2/2}$ lacks an antiderivative expressible in terms of elementary functions (polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions)[cite: 5]. Consequently, standard calculus technique cannot yield a closed-form formula for $\Phi(z)$. To bridge this, mathematics introduces special functions like the Error Function ($\text{erf}$): $$\text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^x e^{-t^2} \, dt$$ Even with $\text{erf}(x)$, practical evaluations require numerical methods such as power series expansions, numerical quadrature, or rational minimax approximations.

Comparison of Numerical Approaches for Normal CDF

Method Mathematical Formulation Strengths & Convergence Limitations
Power Series Expansion $\Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k z^{2k+1}}{2^k k! (2k+1)}$ Extremely fast, exact convergence for small to moderate $|z| \le 3.0$. Alternating series bound[cite: 6]. Suffers from catastrophic floating-point cancellation for large $|z| > 4.5$.
Composite Simpson's Rule Numerical Simpson quadrature over integral range $[0, z]$. High accuracy $O(h^4)$, robust, straightforward implementation. Requires evaluate loops over step grids; higher computational overhead.
Abramowitz & Stegun Rational Approx Polynomial/rational approximation of $\text{erfc}(x)$ (Formula 7.1.26). Ultra-fast $O(1)$ computation; max absolute error $< 1.5 \times 10^{-7}$. Bounded approximation accuracy (fixed polynomial order).
